Types of Ecological Surveys
Professional ecological surveys in Ireland cover a wide range of environments, species, and statutory requirements, often forming part of planning and development processes.
The types of professional ecological surveys can generally be grouped into three main categories: 
Habitat Surveys, 
Species-Specific Surveys
Ecological Assessments/Reports.

1. Habitat Surveys and Mapping
These surveys classify and map the types of habitats present on a site, usually according to the Fossitt classification system (A Guide to Habitats in Ireland).
Preliminary Ecological Appraisal (PEA) / Walkover Survey: A rapid assessment, often the first step, involving a desk study and a site visit to identify habitats present, their ecological value, and any potential for protected species. This often includes an Extended Phase 1 Habitat Survey.
Detailed Habitat Mapping: In-depth mapping and classification of all habitats on a site, often to a detailed Fossitt Level 3, to inform impact assessments or management plans.
Wetland Surveys: Specialised surveys for bogs, fens, swamps, and other wetland habitats, often linked to the conservation of peatlands or compliance with the Water Framework Directive.
Marine and Freshwater Surveys: Surveys of coastal, estuarine, river, and lake environments, including:
Macrophyte Surveys (aquatic plants).
Benthic/Intertidal Surveys (organisms living on or in the seabed/shore).


What are the differences between each of the habitat surveys?

The difference lies primarily in the level of detail, the required reporting structure, and when they occur in the development planning process.
In Ireland, the standard habitat classification system is the Fossitt classification (A Guide to Habitats in Ireland).
Here is an outline comparing the most common types of habitat surveys and assessments:
Survey/Assessment Type
Purpose & Timing
Methodology & Detail
Key Deliverable
1. Preliminary Ecological Appraisal (PEA)
Purpose: The initial screening process. It identifies all potential ecological constraints and opportunities on a site before design plans are finalised.
Scope: Always includes a Desk Study (reviewing existing records, designated sites) AND a Walkover/Field Study (the Extended Phase 1/Fossitt survey).
A PEA Report, which concludes whether further detailed surveys are necessary (the "Phase 2" surveys).
2. Extended Phase 1 Habitat Survey
Purpose: To provide the factual, mapped data required for the PEA/EcIA. It is the field component of the PEA.
Detail: Maps habitats using the Fossitt system (typically to Level 3) and includes Target Notes for features of interest (e.g., potential bat roosts, badger setts). It also assesses habitat suitability for protected species.
A Habitat Map and detailed species list (the foundational data for the report).
3. Phase 2 / Detailed Habitat Surveys
Purpose: To provide highly detailed, quantitative data where the PEA or Extended Phase 1 identified a potentially significant, ecologically valuable, or protected habitat.
Detail: Moves beyond simple classification. Methods often include National Vegetation Classification (NVC), detailed quadrat analysis, and monitoring to understand species composition, condition, and community structure.
Detailed NVC or Irish Vegetation Classification (IVC) reports, often required for high-value sites (e.g., qualifying habitats in a SAC).
4. Specialised Surveys (e.g., Wetland Surveys)
Purpose: To focus exclusively on one type of complex habitat, often required to meet specific legislative requirements (like the Water Framework Directive).
Detail: Uses bespoke methodologies for that habitat type. For wetlands, this includes hydrology assessment, detailed peat probing, and specific sampling techniques for aquatic flora and fauna.
Highly technical report specific to the habitat, usually including condition assessment.

(c) Phase 2 / NVC (National Vegetation Classification) Report
This template is for a detailed, quantitative survey of a high-value habitat, typically following a PEA recommendation. NVC is commonly used in Ireland (often transitioning to the Irish Vegetation Classification - IVC) for legally protected or sensitive habitats.
Section
Content Description
Key Focus
1. Introduction
1.1 Background: State the purpose is to quantitatively classify and map the vegetation communities as recommended by the PEA. 1.2 Survey Objectives: Determine NVC/IVC community types, assess condition against conservation objectives (e.g., Annex I status), and provide detailed data for EcIA mitigation.

2.1 Field Sampling: Details of the Quantitative Sampling technique (e.g., number and size of Quadrats / Relevés used), and the recording of D.A.F.O.R (Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare) or Species Abundance Scales (e.g., National Vegetation Database protocols). 2.2 Analysis: Method for classifying field data to specific NVC/IVC community and sub-community types (e.g., use of keys, statistical software). 2.3 Condition Assessment: Methodology for evaluating the habitat condition (e.g., Common Standards Monitoring - CSM metrics).

4.1 Conservation Status: Interpretation of the NVC results in the context of Irish and EU conservation designations (e.g., confirming if a community is a priority Annex I habitat). 4.2 Implications: Discussion of the habitat's sensitivity and the potential impact of the proposed development. 4.3 Recommendations: Specific mitigation/compensation measures tailored to the identified NVC communities (e.g., hydrological protection for a wetland, seed harvesting for grassland restoration).

2. Species-Specific Surveys
These focus on specific groups of fauna and flora, particularly those that are protected under Irish and European law (e.g., the Wildlife Acts and the EU Habitats and Birds Directives).
Species Group
Common Survey Types
Bats (All species protected)
Preliminary Roost Assessment (PRA) (initial inspection), Emergence/Re-entry Surveys, Activity/Transect Surveys (using bat detectors).
Birds
Breeding Bird Surveys (identifying territories/nests, especially for protected species like Hen Harrier), Wintering Bird Surveys, Seabird Surveys (for coastal/offshore projects).
Mammals
Surveys for evidence (tracks, scats, setts, etc.) of protected mammals like Badger (setts), Otter (holts and spraints), Pine Marten, and Red Squirrel.
Amphibians & Reptiles
Surveys for species like the Smooth Newt and Common Lizard.
Fish & Aquatic Species
Fisheries Surveys (e.g., electrofishing, spawning redd counts), Freshwater Pearl Mussel Surveys, White-clawed Crayfish Surveys.
Invertebrates
Specific surveys for rare or protected insect species, such as the Marsh Fritillary Butterfly.
Flora & Invasive Species
Botanical Surveys (detailed species inventories, focusing on rare or protected plants), and Invasive Alien Species Surveys (e.g., Japanese Knotweed, Rhododendron, Giant Hogweed) and risk assessment.


What are the differences between each of the species Specific surveys?

Protected species in Ireland are highly specialised and must be conducted using specific, standardised methodologies during restricted time windows. The differences between them revolve around the objective of the survey, the time of day/year they are conducted, and the equipment used.
Here is an outline of the differences between the main categories of species-specific surveys:

3. Ecological Assessments and Planning Reports
These documents are often required by planning authorities to evaluate a project's impact on the environment.
Ecological Impact Assessment (EcIA): An evaluation of the likely significant effects of a project on important ecological features, often forming the Biodiversity Chapter of an Environmental Impact Assessment Report (EIAR).
Appropriate Assessment (AA) Screening: The first stage of a process required under the EU Habitats Directive. It determines if a project is likely to have a significant effect on a Natura 2000 site (Special Area of Conservation or Special Protection Area).
Natura Impact Statement (NIS): If the AA screening determines a significant effect cannot be ruled out, a full NIS is prepared to assess the impacts on the integrity of the Natura 2000 site and propose mitigation.
Arboricultural Implication Assessment (AIA) / Tree Surveys: Assessment of the condition and value of trees on a site and the potential impact of development, often referencing the British Standard BS 5837.
Ecological Clerk of Works (ECoW): On-site monitoring and supervision during construction to ensure that environmental and ecological mitigation measures are implemented correctly and in compliance with planning conditions.
What are the differences between each of the Ecological Assessments and Planning surveys?
The core difference between ecological assessments and planning reports in Ireland is their legal basis, scope of coverage, and focus. Some address all biodiversity, while others are strictly limited to EU-protected sites.
Here are the key distinctions between the major assessment and reporting types:

1. Ecological Impact Assessment (EcIA)
The EcIA is the comprehensive ecological impact chapter submitted for a development.
Legal Basis & Scope: It is the Biodiversity Chapter (or Flora and Fauna chapter) of an Environmental Impact Assessment Report (EIAR), which is required by Irish law (transposing the EU EIA Directive) for large-scale or sub-threshold developments.
Focus: It addresses all biodiversity in the site and its Zone of Influence (ZoI). This includes habitats classified using Fossitt, legally protected species under the Wildlife Acts (e.g., Badger, all Bat species, Otter, Pine Marten), and species/habitats of local/regional importance.
Conclusion: It evaluates the significance of residual ecological effects after mitigation.

2. Appropriate Assessment (AA) Process
The AA process is a tiered, legal test that is entirely focused on EU-protected sites. It is driven by the EU Habitats Directive.
A. Appropriate Assessment (AA) Screening
Objective: To determine, using objective scientific criteria, if a plan or project, alone or in combination with other plans/projects, is likely to have a significant effect on a Natura 2000 site (SAC or SPA).
Scope: It considers only the conservation objectives of the relevant Natura 2000 sites. It primarily checks for Source-Pathway-Receptor (S-P-R) links (e.g., a connection via surface water or a shared species territory).
Conclusion: The result is either "Screened Out" (finding of no significant effects, and the process stops) or "Screened In" (significant effects cannot be ruled out, and the process must advance to Stage 2).
B. Natura Impact Statement (NIS)
Objective: The report that enables the Planning Authority to carry out the formal Stage 2 Appropriate Assessment. It is required if the project is "Screened In."
Scope: A detailed, rigorous scientific examination that assesses the adverse effects on the integrity of the Natura 2000 site in view of its conservation objectives. Mitigation measures must be scientifically robust and certain to remove the risk of adverse effects.
Conclusion: The planning authority must conclude "no adverse effect on the integrity of the site" before the project can be permitted.

3. Supplementary Site Role (ECoW)
The Ecological Clerk of Works (ECoW) is a role, not a report, but is often included as a planning requirement derived from the assessment reports.
Objective: To act as the "eyes and ears" of the ecologist on the construction site to ensure compliance with planning conditions and mitigation measures.
Timing: Active during the construction phase (post-planning).
Role: The ECoW supervises sensitive activities (e.g., tree felling, work near watercourses, timing of initial ground works), provides toolbox talks to contractors, and stops work immediately if a protected species or habitat is threatened. They submit compliance reports back to the planning authority.

Key Focus

(b) Appropriate Assessment (AA) Screening Report
This report is the first stage of the legal test under the Habitats Directive. The primary goal is to determine if a full Natura Impact Statement (NIS) is required.
Section
Content Description
Key Conclusion Required
1. Introduction
1.1 Project Description: Summary of the proposed plan or project (name, location, scale). 1.2 Legislative Context: Briefly state the requirement for AA under the Habitats Directive and Irish Regulations. 1.3 Rationale: Confirm the assessment is a Stage 1 Screening and is based on objective information and the Precautionary Principle.

2. Methodology
2.1 Zone of Influence (ZoI): Justify the geographical area searched (typically $15 \text{km}$ for terrestrial sites, extending by hydrological pathways). 2.2 Designated Site Identification: List all Natura 2000 Sites (SACs and SPAs) within the ZoI, including their Site Codes and Qualifying Interests (QIs) / Special Conservation Interests (SCIs). 2.3 Data Sources: List all sources used (NPWS, NBDC, EPA, etc.).

3. Screening Assessment
3.1 Source-Pathway-Receptor Analysis (S-P-R): Systematic analysis for each Natura 2000 site: Is there a Source (potential impact, e.g., silt runoff)? Is there a Pathway (e.g., river connection)? Key Conclusion Required

1.1 Purpose: State that the report provides information for the Competent Authority to carry out the formal Stage 2 Appropriate Assessment. 1.2 Project Details: Detailed description of the project, including design changes made to reduce initial impacts.

GIS data


The key data points, or minimum attribute fields, required for a consistent and interoperable Irish habitat Geographic Information System (GIS) dataset are standardised to ensure ease of storage, retrieval, and comparison across different habitat survey projects.
These data points are stored in an attribute table associated with each spatial feature (polygon, polyline, or point) that represents a habitat patch.
The absolute minimum attribute fields that must be included in each habitat dataset are:

1. OBJECT_ID
    ◦ Description: A unique numeric identifier assigned to each polygon, polyline, or point within the dataset. This field helps maintain the connection between spatial features and their non-spatial descriptive data.
2. FOSS_CODE
    ◦ Description: The alphanumeric habitat code corresponding to the standard Irish habitat classification system, A Guide to Habitats in Ireland (Fossitt, 2000).
3. ANNEX_CODE
    ◦ Description: The alphanumeric habitat code for the relevant Habitats Directive habitat types listed on Annex I (if applicable), as detailed in The Interpretation Manual of European Union Habitats.
4. DATA_QUAL
    ◦ Description: An indication of the field data quality and the source of the habitat record, providing the user with the degree of confidence in the data. Standard codes define the level of survey undertaken, including:
        ▪ S: Field data collected by walkover survey, involving detailed inspection.
        ▪ V: Data have been field validated, viewed in the field in less detail (e.g., viewing from a distance).
        ▪ DA, DB, DC, DD: Codes indicating data derived solely from desktop sources (e.g., high-quality recent data, older high-quality data, interpretation of aerial photography supplemented by other data, or interpretation of aerial photography only, respectively).
5. DATE
    ◦ Description: The date on which the field survey was conducted.
Examples of Recommended Additional Attribute Fields
Depending on the specific objectives and scope of the habitat survey, additional attribute fields are often included to provide more comprehensive information:

It is important that GIS databases are designed to be as simple as possible to permit flexibility in analysis and presentation, ensuring that information is stored in its smallest logical part (e.g., separate fields for habitat code and habitat name).
